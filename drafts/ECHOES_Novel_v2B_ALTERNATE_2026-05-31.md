## PART ONE: THE VOID BETWEEN

---

### The State of Things

By 2175, humanity had not conquered space.

It had negotiated with it.

The terms were unfavorable — they always were — but the species had learned, slowly and at considerable cost, that survival in the void required a particular kind of stubbornness. Not bravery. Not genius. Just the refusal to accept that the conditions were impossible, repeated across generations until the impossible became merely difficult, and the difficult became ordinary, and the ordinary became home.

Mars was home now. Not for everyone. Not comfortably. But home in the way that a place becomes home when enough people are born there and enough people die there and the generations stack up until the original decision to go seems less like a choice and more like a fact of history.

The cities on Mars did not sit under open sky. They sat under domes — vast hexagonal structures of plexiglass panes, each one fifteen centimeters thick and two and a half meters wide, bonded to alloy frames with an epoxy compound that had been refined over decades into something approaching permanence. The panes were joined edge to edge across the full span of the dome, which rose from a perimeter wall ten meters high and one and a half meters thick that circled each city at its boundary like a declaration. Inside the dome you could walk the streets of a city that looked, felt, and smelled like something built on Earth. The light was managed. The temperature was managed. The air was managed. Children grew up in these cities and knew the sky above them was engineered and didn't particularly care, the same way children everywhere have always not particularly cared about the infrastructure that keeps them alive.

At the outer districts, near the wall, you could see the seams between panes. You could look up and count them, hexagon after hexagon, and follow them to the apex of the dome and then back down the other side, and if you stood there long enough in the right light you could see the dark beyond them — not the dark of a cloudy night but the dark of a sky that had no atmosphere to scatter it, a darkness so complete it had weight. Most people who grew up on Mars learned not to look up at the outer districts. Not because it frightened them. Because it reminded them of something they preferred not to think about.

The something was simple: outside the dome, you died in ninety seconds.

Space travel, by 2175, had improved enough to matter. The drives that moved ships through the void were not the drives of a century prior — they were faster, more efficient, less catastrophically expensive to operate. A journey that once took years now took months. This was not magic. It was engineering, accumulated over generations, paid for in failures that nobody put on the brochure. The result was a humanity that could reach the outer solar system in a timeframe that didn't require cryo sleep for the entire voyage. Eight months from Mars to the region of Pluto's orbit was considered an acceptable posting. Not comfortable. But acceptable.

This mattered because of what was out there.

### The Vessel

It had no proper name yet in this telling. Names come later, when things are understood well enough to be called something. For now it was what it had always been to those who didn't live on it: a designation on a corporate manifest, a line item in a budget, a signal on a long-range scan that should have been there and then wasn't.

To the people who lived on it, it was simply the ship. The way a town is simply the town when it's the only one you know.

Construction began in the first quarter of the twenty-first century, in orbit — because it had to be in orbit. The vessel was too large to build under gravity and survive the lifting of it. It was assembled over years from components launched from Earth and later from the earliest Mars facilities, bolted together piece by piece in the patient way that large things get built when there is no alternative to patience. When it was finished it was not beautiful. It was functional, which for something that size was its own kind of beauty. A structure designed to sustain a living, working community for decades without resupply. Self-sufficient in the ways that mattered: power, atmosphere, food production, water cycling, medical care. A city that could move, slowly, through the dark.

It launched in 2093, carrying its first full crew complement, bound for Pluto.

Pluto was not the destination for any romantic reason. Pluto was where the research required it to go — the outer system, far enough from the sun that certain conditions existed nowhere else, close enough to the ecliptic plane that the journey, while long, was calculable. The company that owned and operated the vessel had contracted with several research institutions and government bodies whose specific interests in the outer system were not publicized and were not the business of the general crew. Deep space research was like that. The people who funded it rarely explained themselves to the people who did it.

The journey to Pluto, at the vessel's sustainable cruise speed, would take approximately sixty-seven years.

This was understood from the beginning. Nobody signed on expecting to see Pluto in their lifetime. They signed on for eight-year terms — the minimum period before a rotation ship would come to collect those whose contracts had expired and deliver the next cohort. Eight years was a long time in a small world. Some people did one term and left. Some did two, three, four. Some stopped counting and stopped leaving. The vessel became the thing their lives were built around, the way a city does for the people who grow old in it without particularly meaning to.

Every three to five years a supply ship made the journey. It brought equipment, food stores, replacement parts, medical supplies, new crew, mail from people back on Earth or Mars who were still thinking of someone out here. It brought, occasionally and unofficially, things that weren't on the manifest — small comforts that the long dark made worth the risk of smuggling. Nobody inspected too carefully. The ship's administration understood that a community eight years from the nearest port needed certain allowances to remain functional.

The supply ship would dock, unload, load the outbound crew whose terms had ended, and leave. The journey back took years. By the time news from the vessel reached Earth, it was old news. By the time a response reached the vessel, the situation that prompted it had long since resolved itself one way or another.

This was the texture of life at that distance. You were on your own, in ways that the word didn't quite capture. The people who thrived out there were the ones who made peace with that. Who found the community aboard the vessel to be sufficient. Who stopped measuring their lives against what they'd left behind.

Many of them had nothing to go back to anyway.

By 2143, the vessel was fifty years into its journey. It had carried hundreds of people through its corridors over those decades — scientists, engineers, maintenance workers, medical staff, administrators, and a small number of people who had simply ended up there by a series of decisions that seemed logical at the time. Its population at any given point hovered around one hundred and fifty. A small city's worth of people, moving slowly through the dark toward a destination none of them would live to see.

Three-quarters of the way to Pluto.

That was as far as it got.

### 2143

What happened in 2143 is documented in fragments.

The full picture does not exist in any one place. It exists in pieces — in log entries and sensor records and the preserved data of a ship's systems that kept running long after the people who built them were gone. It exists in the architecture of the disaster itself, in the shape of things left behind, in the empty spaces where things should have been.

What is known: there was research being conducted aboard the vessel that most of its crew knew very little about. This was not unusual. The vessel carried multiple research programs simultaneously, and compartmentalization was standard practice. The one hundred and fifty people who called the vessel home went about their days — maintenance shifts, meal rotations, the small social structures that communities build to make large spaces feel smaller — largely unaware of what was happening in the sealed sections of the research wing. There were whispers, as there always are. Something strange. Something that didn't quite fit the profile of ordinary deep-space science. But whispers in a closed community are easy to dismiss, and most people dismissed them.

The research team at the center of it numbered fourteen. Specialists, brought aboard specifically for this work. They had been on the vessel long enough to be known, to have become part of the fabric of the community, to have faces and habits and histories that other crew members recognized in passing. Whatever they were doing behind the closed doors of their section, they were not strangers.

Something went wrong.

Not all at once. Nothing catastrophic ever happens all at once — it happens in stages, each one survivable in isolation, each one making the next one worse. The vessel had procedures for emergencies. It had redundancies and failsafes and a chain of command designed to manage crisis. These things helped and then they didn't.

By the time the wider crew understood the scale of what they were dealing with, the situation had already progressed beyond what procedures were designed to handle.

The chaos that followed was the kind that happens when a hundred and fifty people in a sealed environment realize simultaneously that their survival is not guaranteed. Resources were contested. Escape was contested. Every decision became a negotiation conducted under conditions that made rational negotiation nearly impossible. People acted out of fear and exhaustion and the particular desperation of knowing that help was years away.

Some survived the initial chaos. Some did not. The escape pod bays saw what they saw — twenty pods, distributed across four bays throughout the vessel, each one built to carry six people away from a ship in crisis. Capacity for one hundred and twenty out of a population of one hundred and fifty. The mathematics of that shortfall played out the way mathematics always plays out: without sentiment.

Between Stardate 2143.162 and 2143.164, pods launched from the bays that people could reach. Not all bays. Not all pods. Chaos is not organized. Some pods launched with fewer than six. Some bays went unreached.

Nine days before the first pod launched, at Stardate 2143.159, the vessel's outgoing transmission systems went offline.

Not from damage. Not from power failure.

Someone turned them off.

The pods that left the vessel left in silence. Their emergency beacons, their distress signals, their navigation transponders — all of them subject to the same system that had been disabled. They drifted away from the vessel and disappeared into the dark the way things disappear at that distance: completely, and without announcement.

The vessel went dark.

The company waited for a routine communication that never came. Then another. They revised their expectations downward: communication failure at extreme range was not unprecedented. The vessel's self-sufficiency meant it didn't need to report regularly to continue functioning. A supply run was several years out. They would know more then.

They noted the anomaly in the operational log.

They moved on.

### The Years Between

Thirty-two years is a long time for a secret to keep itself.

The vessel drifted on its original heading — still pointed at Pluto, still moving, still running on the minimal power its surviving systems could maintain. From the outside, it was indistinguishable from a vessel that had simply gone quiet. Deep space had a way of swallowing things. Ships went dark. Signals decayed. The distance between a vessel at thirty astronomical units and the nearest inhabited body was measured in years of light, in decades of radio. The company restructured twice. Changed hands once. The people who had filed the anomaly report were mostly retired or dead.

The escaped pods drifted in silence somewhere in the outer system, each one carrying whoever had made it aboard, sealed in cryo sleep that would hold for perhaps fifty years at full power before the systems began to fail. They had no signal. They had no way to be found. They were simply out there — suspended, waiting, unknowing.

Thirty-two years passed.

Then, in 2175, the vessel appeared on long-range scans.

Not reappeared. Appeared — as though for the first time, as though it had been invisible and had chosen to stop being invisible. A return signal where there had been none. A location where only silence had been. The scan data was cross-referenced against registry records and matched to the vessel's identification signature, flagged years prior as dark, status unknown.

A company called Hugo Avila.

Hugo called a crew.

*Stardate 2175.150.0213. Eight months out from Mars.*

### II. THE CROSSING

The dock corridor between vessels was forty feet of pressurized tube. The facility's airlock accepted their override on the first attempt — no resistance, no alarm. It simply opened.

Which was either good or the kind of bad that didn't bother announcing itself.

Emergency strips ran along the corridor floor — pale amber, low draw, just enough to navigate by. The hum that every inhabited vessel had, that constant low frequency that became indistinguishable from silence after the first few days — gone. The facility's silence was a different thing from ordinary quiet. It had mass.

> **COOPER:** *(low)* This is fine. Completely normal abandoned space facility.

Nobody replied. They moved.

The corridor was thirty-two years of undisturbed dust on every surface not in the foot traffic path they were making. The air was old — stale, flat, the atmosphere of a sealed space that had been breathing itself for decades. It was breathable. It just didn't feel like it wanted to be.

### IV. DARK

### V. INTO THE DARK

The low grid ended at the corridor threshold like a line someone had drawn on purpose. Beyond it — the kind of dark that a kit light made a narrow tunnel through and nothing more.

> **JAIME:** *(checking the map)* Branch left takes us toward Cargo and Engineering. Right runs parallel to Crew Quarters. Straight on is the longer route to the rear grid.

> **JASON:** Crew Quarters didn't register on the sensor read. We stay off that section until we know more. We go straight.

Nobody argued.

The corridor widened briefly at a transit junction — a lift lobby, doors dark, no indicator. A supply cart abandoned against the wall, halfway loaded, one wheel still ticking from the low-power vibration in the deck plates.

> **COOPER:** *(passing)* Someone left in a hurry.

> **ZEKE:** Or didn't leave at all.

### VII. CARGO

*The door to the cargo bay wasn't open.*

*The door to the cargo bay was gone.*

*Not forced. Removed. Left leaning against the corridor wall like someone needed the opening wider and hadn't come back to put it right.*

---

The space inside was large — shelving units in rows, floor to ceiling. Some fully stocked. Others stripped bare. A utility dolly on its side in the main aisle, one wheel ticking against the deck from the low-power vibration in the plating.

> **COOPER:** *(watching the wheel)* That can't still be moving.

> **ZEKE:** Settlement. Deck plates flex on low power.

> **COOPER:** Sure.

Jaime moved to the nearest stocked shelf. Sealed containers, equipment housings, expired food stores in vacuum seal. He read the pattern of what was missing.

> **JAIME:** They took what they could carry. Prioritized anything hand-portable.

> **JASON:** Or they ran out of time to come back.

In the third side aisle — a sleeping bag. Rolled out in the gap between two shelving units, just wide enough for a person. A water container. A ration pack, opened, partially eaten, set aside to be finished later.

Nobody finished it later.

> **JASON:** More than once. The scuff marks.

> **JAIME:** Did they make it to a pod?

### VIII. CREW QUARTERS

*The corridor narrowed. Ceiling dropped. Infrastructure simplified. This part of the vessel had been built for people to live in.*

*Four doors on each side. Most closed. Two open.*

---

Jason held up a hand. They stopped.

Nothing moved. The low grid cast pale light on eight doors and the quiet of thirty-two years settled into the walls.

> **JASON:** Two minutes. Don't go past the doorframe alone.

He and Jaime took the first open door left. Zeke and Cesar took the one across. Cooper stayed at the junction, hand resting on the grip of his pistol. Not drawing. Just resting.

*Inside the first quarter: two bunks. One made with precision. The other slept in and left — sheets pulled back, pillow still carrying the impression of someone's head. A mug on the desk, something long-dried in the bottom. A photograph on the wall. Two people. Somewhere with sunlight.*

Jason looked at the mug. Looked at the photograph.

> **JASON:** *(low, to himself)* Someone made their bed every morning. Someone else didn't.

Across the corridor, Jaime stood in the open doorway without going in. Zeke was at the desk, checking a dead personal device.

> **JAIME:** They had full lives here. Pictures. Coffee. Routines.

> **ZEKE:** Forty-seven days from the nearest port. They weren't going home soon.

> **JAIME:** Do you think they had any warning?

> **ZEKE:** The door came down in cargo on 2143.169. Pods launched 2143.162 to 2143.164. Whatever happened, it moved fast at first. Then lasted a long time after.

> **JAIME:** The person in the cargo bay. To at least 2143.181.

> **ZEKE:** Minimum.

> **COOPER:** *(from down the corridor)* Hey. There's something here.

*At the far end, in a maintenance alcove: a personal kit bag. Packed. Zipped. Set carefully against the wall. A faded name tab on the shoulder strap. Next to it, tucked under the strap, a child's drawing. Folded twice.*

*Nobody unfolded it.*

> **COOPER:** *(long moment, no humor in it)* I don't like this place.

> **JAIME:** I know.

> **JASON:** Mark the section. We move.

Cooper looked at the bag one more time. Adjusted the grip of his pistol — same absent gesture, same confirmation — and turned back.

> **COOPER:** Yeah. Okay.

### IX. THE REAR POWER SUPPLY

*The blast doors at the end of the final corridor were cracked open — just enough for one person at a time. The manual override lever on the frame was bent. Used hard. From the inside.*

*Above the doors, the amber indicator pulsed in a long, slow rhythm.*

*Something back there was still alive.*

> **JASON:** There it is.

> **CESAR:** Single file. Weapons ready — don't draw unless I say.

*They slipped through the gap one at a time and into the rear power supply.*

*Industrial. Red-amber emergency lighting. Conduit and housing everywhere. At the center: the primary distribution hub, twelve feet high, indicator panels running its full height. Most dark. Some amber. Three at the top — green.*

*Still running.*

Jason was already at the column.

> **JAIME:** *(raising his kit light)* What are we looking at?

> **JASON:** Hub's intact. No physical damage. Those three stacks at the top have been running the low grid and life support. Everything below them is dark because there's nothing feeding it.

He crouched at the base and popped the access panel. Four cell housings. All empty.

> **JASON:** They pulled the primary cells.

> **ZEKE:** *(from the secondary panel)* Same here. Secondary feed is empty too.

> **JASON:** *(opening comms)* Maritza — hub is intact. Primary and secondary cell housings are empty. What does that do to the sub-grids?

> **MARITZA** *(comms)*: They're dead without new power sources. The hub is just a distributor. You need to feed it fuel cells. Should be distributed in storage throughout the vessel — engineering bays, cargo, maintenance.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: So you need to find fuel cells on a vessel that's been dark for thirty-two years.

> **JASON:** That's what it sounds like.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Hugo's not going to —

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Robert.

Silence.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Jason. Is it doable?

Jason stood. Looked at the empty housings. Looked up to the three green indicators still running at the top.

> **JASON:** The vessel's been running on whatever was left in those top stacks for thirty-two years. That means the cell design holds charge for an extremely long time under low draw. If there are spares on this ship — and a vessel this size, there should be — they're not going to be dead just from sitting.

> **GEORGE:** And the dock release?

> **JASON:** Two, maybe three cells minimum to get there. More for full power.

> **COOPER:** *(from the blast door gap)* So we're going deeper into the ship.

> **JASON:** Yeah.

> **COOPER:** Great.

Zeke had something on the diagnostic panel.

> **ZEKE:** Jason. The cells weren't depleted. Look at the draw history — hard cutoff. Not a drain. They were removed.

> **JASON:** *(crossing the room, reading the panel)* Stardate 2143.158.

> **ZEKE:** One day before the transmissions were cut.

> **JASON:** Someone knew what was coming. Pulled the power before the pods, before the transmissions.

> **JAIME:** To stop something from running?

> **ZEKE:** Or to use it somewhere else.

> **JASON:** Destination is corrupted. But they went somewhere on this vessel. Which means they're still here.

Three questions. Who. Why. Where.

> **JASON:** We go back through what we've cleared. Rest, eat, plan the next section. We're not going deeper without knowing what we're going into.

> **COOPER:** First smart thing said in the last hour.

> **JASON:** *(opening comms)* George — heading back to the Operations Center. Hold tight. We have a plan. It's going to take longer than we'd like.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Copy. We're not going anywhere.

> **GEORGE** *(comms — quieter)*: Good work out there.

*They filed back through the blast door gap. The hub stood behind them in red-amber light, three green indicators running at the top, four empty housings at the base.*

*The cells were somewhere on the vessel.*

*The vessel watched them go.*

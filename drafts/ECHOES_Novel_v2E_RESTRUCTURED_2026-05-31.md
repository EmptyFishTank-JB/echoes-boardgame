# ECHOES
### A Novel

## PART ONE: ARRIVAL

---

### I. THE APPROACH

*Stardate 2175.150.0213. Eight months out from Mars.*
*The derelict vessel. Dark. Enormous. No running lights, no beacon, no sign of cycle.*
*A structure designed to sustain one hundred and fifty people indefinitely,*
*sitting dead in the black like someone had simply walked away from it mid-sentence.*

---

Jaime Rodriguez keyed the comms for the third time. Same tone as the first two — steady, professional, the kind of voice that didn't telegraph anxiety even when it had good reason to.

> **JAIME:** This is survey vessel on approach — please respond on standard or emergency channel. Registered salvage crew. Respond at your convenience.

Static. Long and flat.

> **COOPER:** "At your convenience." You're very polite to a dark ship, man.

> **JAIME:** You never know.

> **COOPER:** I feel like in this specific case we know.

Ezekiel Tagle was at the sensor array. He'd been quiet since they came into visual range, which meant he was actually thinking.

> **ZEKE:** Hull reads intact. Primary dock corridor is pressurized. Internal atmosphere — present. Most of the facility.

> **JAIME:** Most?

> **ZEKE:** A few sections aren't reading. Could be isolation seals. Could be something else. I'd need to be inside to tell the difference.

Cesar Roque set down his tablet and looked at the viewport.

> **CESAR:** Automated emergency broadcast should be transmitting regardless of crew status. It isn't. That's the part that doesn't fit.

> **COOPER:** So the backup plan also stopped. That's a great sign.

> **CESAR:** It isn't.

> **COOPER:** I know. I was being sarcastic.

> **CESAR:** I know. I was being accurate.

From the comms board, Robert Tijerina came through — calm and measured in the particular way that meant he'd been sitting with something and decided the moment had come to weigh in.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: What's the read from out there? Hugo's directive flagged automated systems as a priority check before we dock.

> **CESAR:** Emergency broadcast is inactive. Atmosphere and hull are nominal. We're assessing.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Copy. Jason — you've seen dark configurations before. Anything about this that concerns you?

Jason Betancourt was at the secondary panel, running the docking approach calculations he'd already run twice.

> **JASON:** The broadcast being down is the only thing that doesn't add up. Everything else looks like a passive shutdown, not a catastrophic one. We won't know more until we're in.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Right, good. That's the expert read I was looking for.

George Castañeda shifted at the secondary comms board.

> **GEORGE:** I've got nothing on return signal. Still just noise.

> **COOPER:** Were you asleep?

> **GEORGE:** I was conserving energy. It's a long approach.

> **LESSLY** *(from somewhere behind him)*: He was asleep.

> **GEORGE:** *(not denying it)* I heard everything.

Maritza Garza leaned against the far bulkhead, scrolling through facility schematics on her tablet without looking up.

> **MARITZA:** The mechanical systems on this thing are going to be a disaster. Fourteen months dark, no cycle maintenance — whatever's still running in there, it's running wrong.

> **LESSLY:** Are we factoring that into the approach plan or just noting it?

> **MARITZA:** *(still not looking up)* Noting it. Nobody asked me about the approach plan.

> **LESSLY:** Nobody asked me either.

> **MARITZA:** Welcome to the team.

Then, from behind them:

> **ROBERT:** Alright. Let's go over the directive one more time so we're all aligned.

*Nobody turned around immediately.*

> **ROBERT:** Hugo was clear — primary objective is resource recovery, secondary is facility assessment, minimal risk posture throughout. He expects a status report before we lose relay range, which —

> **CESAR:** We know the directive, Robert.

> **ROBERT:** I'm just making sure we're all on the same page. That's part of my role here.

> **CESAR:** We read it. Same as you.

> **ROBERT:** Right, and as the operations lead on this —

> **CESAR:** *(evenly)* The boarding team is Jason, Jaime, Cooper, Zeke, and myself. The plan is to dock, assess the facility entrance, and proceed from the airlock. We've reviewed it.

> **ROBERT:** Yeah, absolutely, and I think that's a solid approach — though I'd maybe recommend we consider —

> **CESAR:** We're docking, Robert.

Silence. Robert adjusted the tablet in his hands.

> **ROBERT:** Hugo also wanted me to flag that if we encounter any technical anomalies — systems, power, anything like that — we defer to the *experts* on the team rather than making field calls independently.

> **COOPER:** *(quietly, to Zeke)* The experts.

> **ZEKE:** *(same volume)* I heard it too.

> **ROBERT:** Jason, you've seen more derelict configurations than anyone, so if something comes up you'd be the one to —

> **JASON:** I know.

> **ROBERT:** Right, exactly, which is why —

> **JASON:** Robert. I know.

Another silence. George coughed. Lessly found something very interesting to look at on her panel.

> **MARITZA:** *(finally looking up)* The auxiliary power grid's in the rear section of the facility. If anything's still cycling it's coming from there.

> **JASON:** Good to know. Thanks, Maritza.

> **MARITZA:** Sure.

> **ROBERT:** See, that's exactly the kind of expert insight that —

> **MARITZA:** *(flat)* Robert, I already have one person I didn't ask to work for. You don't need to compliment me for reading a schematic.

*George made a short sound that might have been a laugh.*

> **GEORGE:** *(to no one in particular)* Every time.

> **LESSLY:** Every time.

The docking alert chimed. Green across the board.

> **CESAR:** We're up. Jaime, Jason, Zeke — dock corridor in three. Cooper, you're with us.

> **COOPER:** *(exhales)* Yeah. I'm with you.

> **JAIME:** *(clapping him once on the shoulder)* It's fine.

> **COOPER:** You don't know that.

> **JAIME:** Not yet.

> **ROBERT:** *(calling after them)* Keep comms open. I'll be coordinating from here. If you need guidance on anything —

*Cesar stopped. Turned back. Looked at Robert — not long, not heated. Just the kind of look that filed something away. Then turned back around and kept walking.*

> **GEORGE:** *(once they were gone, quieter)* You all be careful in there.

*The airlock sealed.*

### — INTERLUDE: THE VESSEL —

*What they had docked against was not a ship in the conventional sense. It was a city that could move.*

*Built in orbit — it had to be, there was no other way — assembled piece by piece over years from components launched from Earth and later from the earliest Mars facilities. When it was finished it was not beautiful. It was functional, which for something that size was its own kind of beauty. Designed to sustain a living community for decades without resupply. Self-sufficient in every way that mattered.*

*It launched in 2093, bound for Pluto. The journey at its sustainable cruise speed would take sixty-seven years. Nobody signed on expecting to see the destination. They signed on for eight-year terms, rotating in and out every three to five years when a supply ship made the trip. Some stayed longer. Some stopped leaving. The vessel became home the way places do when you stop counting the years.*

*At thirty astronomical units from the Sun — three-quarters of the way to Pluto, fifty years into a sixty-seven-year journey — it went dark.*

*That was in 2143.*

*Nobody came for thirty-two years.*

---

### II. THE CROSSING

The dock corridor between vessels was forty feet of pressurized tube. The facility's airlock accepted their override on the first attempt — no resistance, no alarm. It simply opened.

Which was either good or the kind of bad that didn't bother announcing itself.

Emergency strips ran along the corridor floor — pale amber, low draw, just enough to navigate by. The hum that every inhabited vessel had, that constant low frequency you stopped noticing after the first day — gone. The facility's silence was a different thing from ordinary quiet. It had mass.

> **COOPER:** *(low)* This is fine. Completely normal abandoned space facility.

Nobody replied. They moved.

The corridor was thirty-two years of undisturbed dust on every surface not in their path. The air was old — stale, flat, the atmosphere of a sealed space that had been breathing itself for decades. Breathable. It just didn't feel like it wanted to be.

---

### III. THE COMMAND CENTER

*The Operations Center was wide and tiered, workstations arranged in arcs facing a primary display wall that had been dark for thirty-two years. At the center of the room, raised on a platform: the main terminal. One indicator light pulsed on its housing. Slow. Patient.*

*One hundred and fifty people had worked on this vessel.*

*None of them were here.*

---

Cesar walked the perimeter first — exits, entry points, adjacencies. He did that in every room, automatically. The others spread naturally: Jaime at a side workstation, trying for local logs. Zeke straight to the terminal. Jason taking in the room.

> **ZEKE:** It's active. Protected state, but the interface is live. It's been waiting for input.

> **CESAR:** What does it want?

> **ZEKE:** A user.

Cesar looked at Jason.

> **CESAR:** What's your read?

> **JASON:** Could be a standard idle lock. Touch it and it either opens a guest layer or asks for credentials. Either way we learn something. Could also be a trip — depends on how the system was configured when it went down.

> **CESAR:** *(a beat)* Zeke — try the guest layer. Don't push past any access prompts. Anything that asks for credentials, you stop and we read it first.

> **ZEKE:** Got it.

Comms crackled.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Status update. Hugo wanted a check-in before you interact with anything in the Operations Center.

> **CESAR:** We're at the main terminal. About to try guest access. Atmosphere is stable, room is clear.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Copy. Hugo flagged automated security — directive was not to trigger any lockout protocols.

> **CESAR:** We're aware.

> **COOPER:** *(to Jason, low)*: Every time.

> **JASON:** *(same volume)*: Every time.

Zeke touched the terminal.

*The display came alive. Flickered. Resolved. Text populated line by line — deliberate, like something waking up and choosing its words.*

```

v4.2.6

WELCOME BACK.

LAST SESSION:   11,688 DAYS AGO
LAST USER:      [REDACTED]
LAST INPUT:     [REDACTED]

PERSONNEL DETECTED:   5
AUTHORIZATION:        UNVERIFIED

INITIATING FACILITY STATUS REPORT...
```

> **JAIME:** *(watching the screen)* It's been counting days.

> **ZEKE:** It logged us the moment we crossed the airlock. Probably before.

The status report ran. Power levels, atmospheric data, system states. Then:

```
PERSONNEL — ORIGINAL MANIFEST:   150
PERSONNEL — CURRENT:             [CLASSIFIED]
DISCREPANCY:                     LOGGED
CAUSE:                           ARCHIVED
ARCHIVE ACCESS:                  RESTRICTED — CLEARANCE REQUIRED
```

> **COOPER:** Current personnel — classified. What does that mean?

> **JASON:** It means the system knows something it isn't telling us.

> **CESAR:** Zeke, pull the archive access prompt. Let's see what clearance level it's asking for before we do anything else.

Zeke reached for the input field.

The screen changed.

```
UNAUTHORIZED ESCALATION DETECTED.

INITIATING LOCKDOWN PROTOCOL.

THIS IS NOT A WARNING.
```

### IV. DARK

The lights went out.

Not dim. Not flicker. Out — every display, every emergency strip, every indicator on every panel, simultaneously, completely. The ventilation stopped. The low background hum of the facility confirmed itself as gone.

Four seconds of absolute dark and absolute silence.

> **COOPER:** *(somewhere in the black)* Nobody move.

> **JAIME:** I'm here — I've got a light —

Kit lights came on. Three beams finding each other, finding the walls, finding the terminal — dark now, no pulse.

> **ZEKE:** Full shutdown. Not protected state. Root-level kill.

> **CESAR:** *(already on comms)* George — status.

> **GEORGE** *(comms — different now)*: We lost everything. Power, displays, dock controls. All of it, same moment. We're on backup air.

> **MARITZA** *(comms, background)*: Mechanical grid is completely dead. I've got nothing.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: What happened in there? The directive said —

> **CESAR:** Robert. Dock release status.

> **ROBERT:** Not responding. Lessly —

> **LESSLY** *(comms)*: Clamps are locked. Can't release from our side. Has to come from the facility or we need full power to override. We're not going anywhere.

Silence. On both ends.

Jason had moved to the nearest wall panel without being asked, reading it by kit light the way you read something when the display isn't an option.

> **JASON:** *(to Cesar)* The docking protocol pulled power from our ship. Standard handshake — fuel cell charge cycle. Nothing connected on the data side. Power was drawn anyway. Our ship drained in the attempt.

> **CESAR:** So it wasn't targeted.

> **JASON:** Effect is the same. We're not going anywhere until we restore enough of the facility's grid to run the clamp sequence.

Then — slowly — the emergency grid came back. Lower than anything they'd seen. A pale, cold blue-white trace along ceiling seams and floor edges. Life support. Minimal lighting. Communications.

That was all.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Low power's back. Air, lights, comms. Dock release still locked.

> **MARITZA** *(comms)*: I need the mechanical grid before I can do anything from here.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Okay. What's the move?

> **CESAR:** We find the facility's rear power supply, assess what it needs, and restore enough grid to release the clamps. Can't do that from where you are.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: You can do that?

> **JASON:** We can work the problem. That's all I'll tell you right now.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Right. Hugo's going to want —

> **CESAR:** Robert. Stay on channel. Stay calm. We'll report when we have something.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: ...Copy.

> **GEORGE** *(comms — quieter)*: Hey. You all be careful.

> **CESAR:** We will, George.

### — INTERLUDE: 2143 —

*What happened on this vessel is documented in fragments.*

*The full picture does not exist in any one place. It exists in log entries, in sensor records, in the preserved data of systems that kept running long after the people who built them were gone. It exists in the architecture of the disaster — in the shape of things left behind, in the empty spaces where things should have been.*

*There was research being conducted that most of the one hundred and fifty people on board knew very little about. This was not unusual. The vessel carried multiple research programs simultaneously, and compartmentalization was standard practice. The crew went about their days — maintenance shifts, meal rotations, the small social structures that communities build to make large spaces feel smaller — largely unaware of what was happening in the sealed sections of the research wing.*

*There were whispers. There always are.*

*The research team at the center of it numbered fourteen. Specialists. Whatever they were doing behind those closed doors, they were not strangers to the community around them. They had faces, habits, histories.*

*Something went wrong.*

*Not all at once. It never happens all at once.*

### V. INTO THE DARK

The low grid ended at the corridor threshold like a line someone had drawn on purpose. Beyond it — the kind of dark that a kit light made a narrow tunnel through and nothing more.

Cesar went first.

They moved single file. Cesar with his light forward, Jason behind him, Jaime running the map on his kit screen, Zeke at the rear. Cooper fell in between, which put him exactly in the middle.

> **JAIME:** *(low, checking the map)* Branch left takes us toward Cargo and Engineering. Right runs parallel to Crew Quarters. Straight on is the longer route to the rear grid.

> **CESAR:** Any of those sections flagged on the sensor read?

> **JAIME:** Cargo and Engineering are partial. Crew Quarters didn't register at all.

> **JASON:** That's the section that wasn't on the low grid.

> **CESAR:** We go straight. We don't open doors we don't have to.

The corridor widened briefly at a transit junction — a lift lobby, doors closed and dark. Against the wall, a supply cart abandoned, halfway loaded, one wheel ticking against the deck in slow oscillation from the low-power vibration in the plating.

> **COOPER:** *(passing)* Someone left in a hurry.

> **ZEKE:** Or didn't leave at all.

Cooper kept walking.

Further on, the low grid resumed intermittently. A door to the left stood open — panel green, opened from inside and left that way.

Cesar stopped at it. Looked at Jason.

> **CESAR:** What's your read?

> **JASON:** Engineering or Maintenance, based on position. Could have what we need to assess the power grid. Could also tell us what happened in here.

> **CESAR:** We go in. Everybody stays in the threshold sightline — nobody past ten feet until I say.

A maintenance bay. Tool racks along the walls, most still stocked. A workbench with a personal tablet face-down. One overhead panel partially collapsed from thirty-two years of no one to maintain it.

Zeke crossed to the workbench. Tried the tablet. Dead battery.

> **ZEKE:** Dead.

> **JAIME:** Most of this is still here. They weren't looting supplies when it happened.

> **JASON:** Whatever it was, it wasn't slow enough to pack.

He moved to the primary systems panel on the far wall. Display dark, but one physical indicator — amber.

His hand moved briefly to the holster at his hip without thinking. Old habit. He moved on.

> **JASON:** This section's on the grid. Rear supply is still running something. We find it, there's something to work with.

> **CESAR:** Good enough. Let's keep moving.

> **COOPER:** *(from the threshold)* Are we not going to talk about the fact that the door was already open?

> **CESAR:** It was left open.

> **COOPER:** By who?

A beat.

> **CESAR:** That's what we're trying to find out.

They moved on. Corridor, sealed cargo bay they didn't open, another junction, a long straight stretch the kit lights couldn't fully reach.

Comms crackled.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Still with us?

> **CESAR:** Still moving. Nothing to report yet.

> **MARITZA** *(comms)*: Rear supply feeds two sub-grids. Get either one back and you might have enough for dock function. Might.

> **JASON:** What does it take to bring one online?

> **MARITZA:** Without knowing what killed it? Could be a manual reset. Could be hardware. Could be something else.

> **JASON:** Could be something else.

> **MARITZA:** That's the one I'd prepare for.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: I want to flag — Hugo's going to ask for a status report —

> **CESAR:** Robert, when we have something to report we'll report it.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: ...Copy.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: You'll find it. Go on.

The rear section opened ahead — low grid resuming at something approaching usable. Pressure doors at the far end, closed, panel green.

Another door left on open-ready.

> **ZEKE:** *(stopping)* The airlock accepted our override on the first attempt. The Operations Center terminal was in active state — not sleep. Active. The guest layer came up before I asked for it.

> **JASON:** You're saying it was already running.

> **ZEKE:** Everything we've touched in here was already on. Not powered up when we arrived. Already on. Waiting for input.

A long silence.

> **CESAR:** Does that change what we do right now?

> **ZEKE:** No. I just think we should know what we walked into.

> **CESAR:** *(a beat)* Noted.

He pressed the panel. The doors split open.

### — INTERLUDE: THE YEARS BETWEEN —

*The pods that left the vessel left in silence.*

*Their emergency beacons, their distress signals, their navigation transponders — all of them subject to the same system that had been disabled nine days before the first pod launched. They drifted away from the vessel and disappeared into the dark the way things disappear at that distance: completely, and without announcement.*

*Standard emergency cryo systems hold for approximately fifty years at full power before the mechanisms begin to fail. It had been thirty-two.*

*The math of that is simple. The weight of it is not.*

*Somewhere in the outer system, between thirty astronomical units from the Sun and wherever the currents of deep space had taken them, pods drifted in silence. Each one carrying whoever had made it aboard. Sealed. Unknowing. Waiting for rescue that had no way of finding them.*

*Some of the pods had malfunctioned. The void is patient and maintenance requires a living crew.*

*Some had not.*

*Thirty-two years passed. The vessel stayed dark. Then, in 2175, it appeared on long-range scans — not reappeared, appeared — as though it had chosen to stop being invisible. A company called Hugo Avila. Hugo called a crew.*

*Eight months from Mars.*

*Stardate 2175.150.0213.*

### VII. CARGO

*The door to the cargo bay wasn't open.*

*The door to the cargo bay was gone.*

*Not forced. Removed from its track and left leaning against the corridor wall, deliberately, like someone had needed the opening wider and hadn't come back to put it right.*

---

The space inside was large — shelving units in rows, floor to ceiling. Some fully stocked. Others stripped bare. Others partially emptied, remaining contents pushed aside, knocked over. A utility dolly on its side in the main aisle, one wheel ticking against the deck from the low-power vibration in the plating.

> **COOPER:** *(watching the wheel)* That can't still be moving.

> **ZEKE:** Settlement. Deck plates flex on low power.

> **COOPER:** Sure.

Jaime moved to the nearest intact shelf, reading the pattern of what was missing.

> **JAIME:** They took what they could carry. Anything hand-portable is gone. Anything that needed the dolly is still here.

> **JASON:** Or they ran out of time to come back.

> **JAIME:** Yeah. Or that.

Cesar moved through the main aisle. At the third side aisle he stopped.

> **CESAR:** In here.

*A sleeping bag. Rolled out between two shelving units in a gap just wide enough for a person. A water container. A ration pack opened, half-eaten, set aside with the intention of finishing it later.*

*Nobody had finished it later.*

> **COOPER:** Someone was hiding in here.

> **JASON:** More than once. Look at the scuff marks.

The marks were clear in the kit light — the kind that built up over days of the same movement.

> **JAIME:** Did they make it to a pod?

No answer.

Jason moved to the far wall. Generator housings, both dead. His hand moved briefly to his holster — the same absent gesture, the same quiet confirmation — and moved on.

> **JASON:** Generator housings back here — both dead. Restore the rear grid and we can cycle them.

> **CESAR:** Note it.

### VIII. CREW QUARTERS

*The corridor narrowed. The ceiling dropped. The infrastructure simplified. This part of the vessel had been built for people to live in rather than work in. That distinction settled over the group as they moved into it, without anyone naming it.*

*Four doors on each side. Most closed. Two open.*

---

Cesar held up a hand. They stopped.

Nothing moved. The low grid cast pale light on eight doors and thirty-two years of accumulated quiet.

> **CESAR:** Two minutes. Don't go past the doorframe alone.

They split. Cesar and Jason took the first open door left. Zeke and Jaime took the one across. Cooper stayed at the junction, hand resting on the grip of his pistol — not drawing. Just resting. The thing you did in a corridor when everyone else was in a room.

*Inside the first quarter: two bunks. One made with the precision of long habit. The other slept in and not remade. A mug on the desk, something long-dried in the bottom. A photograph on the wall. Two people. Somewhere with sunlight.*

Jason looked at the mug. Looked at the photograph. Didn't touch either.

> **JASON:** *(low)* Someone made their bed every morning. Someone else didn't.

> **CESAR:** Thirty-two years and that's still the first thing you notice.

> **JASON:** Tells you something about who they were.

Across the corridor, Jaime stood in the doorway without going in. Zeke was at the desk, checking a personal device. Dead battery.

> **JAIME:** They had full lives here. Pictures. Coffee. Bad habits.

> **ZEKE:** Forty-seven days from the nearest port, based on the mission profile. They weren't going home soon.

> **JAIME:** You think they had any warning?

> **ZEKE:** The door came down in cargo on 2143.169. Pods launched 2143.162 to 2143.164. Whatever happened, it moved fast at first and then lasted a long time after.

> **JAIME:** The person in the cargo bay. To at least 2143.181.

> **ZEKE:** Minimum.

> **JAIME:** Okay.

He stepped back from the doorframe.

> **COOPER:** *(from down the corridor, quiet)* Hey. There's something here.

*At the far end, in a maintenance alcove: a personal kit bag. Packed. Zipped. Set carefully against the wall. A faded name tab on the shoulder strap. Next to it, tucked under the strap: a child's drawing. Folded twice.*

*Nobody unfolded it.*

> **COOPER:** *(long moment, no humor in it)* I don't like this place.

> **JAIME:** I know.

> **CESAR:** Mark the section. We keep moving.

Cooper looked at the bag one more time. Adjusted the grip of his pistol — same absent gesture — and turned back.

> **COOPER:** Yeah. Okay.

### IX. THE REAR POWER SUPPLY

*The blast doors at the end of the final corridor were cracked open — just enough for one person at a time. The manual override lever on the frame was bent. Used hard. From the inside.*

*Above them, the amber indicator pulsed in a long, slow rhythm.*

*Something back there was still running.*

---

> **JASON:** *(seeing the indicator)* There it is.

> **CESAR:** Single file. Jason, you're behind me. Weapons ready — don't draw unless I say.

*They slipped through the gap one at a time and into the rear power supply.*

*Industrial. Red-amber emergency lighting. Conduit and housing everywhere. At the center: the primary distribution hub — a column of stacked components twelve feet high, indicator panels running its full height. Most dark. Some amber. Three at the very top — green.*

*Still running. Still waiting.*

Jason was already at the column.

> **JAIME:** *(raising his kit light)* What are we looking at?

> **JASON:** Hub's intact. No physical damage. Those three stacks at the top have been running the low grid and life support. Everything below them is dark because there's nothing feeding it.

He crouched at the base. Popped the access panel. Inside: four cell housings. All empty. Contacts clean. Housing intact.

> **JASON:** They pulled the primary cells.

> **ZEKE:** *(from the secondary panel)* Same here. Secondary feed is empty too.

> **CESAR:** Maritza — hub is intact. Primary and secondary cell housings are empty. What does that mean for the sub-grids?

> **MARITZA** *(comms)*: They're not coming back without new power sources. The hub is just a distributor. You need to feed it fuel cells — should be distributed in storage throughout the vessel. Engineering bays, cargo, maintenance.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: So you need to find fuel cells on a vessel that's been dark for thirty-two years.

> **CESAR:** That's what it sounds like.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Hugo's not going to —

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Robert.

Silence.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Jason. Is it doable?

Jason stood. Looked at the empty housings. Looked up the full height of the hub to the three green indicators still running at the top.

> **JASON:** The vessel's been running on whatever was left in those top stacks for thirty-two years. That means the cell design holds charge for an extremely long time under low draw. If there are spares on this ship — and a vessel this size, there should be — they're not going to be dead just from sitting.

> **GEORGE:** And the dock release?

> **JASON:** Two, maybe three cells minimum to get there.

> **GEORGE:** How many for full power and clear?

> **JASON:** More than three.

Silence on the comms.

> **COOPER:** *(from the blast door gap)* So we're going deeper into the ship.

It wasn't a question.

> **JASON:** Yeah.

> **COOPER:** Great.

Zeke had something on the diagnostic panel. He read it twice before speaking.

> **ZEKE:** Jason. The cells weren't depleted. Look at the draw history — hard cutoff. Not a drain. They were removed.

Jason crossed the room. Read the panel.

> **JASON:** Stardate 2143.158.

> **ZEKE:** One day before the transmissions were cut.

The room went quiet in a different way than before.

> **CESAR:** Someone pulled the primary power cells on 2143.158. Transmissions went down on 2143.159. Pods launched 2143.162 through 2143.164.

> **ZEKE:** Someone knew what was coming. Before the pods. Before the transmissions. They pulled the power deliberately.

> **JAIME:** *(carefully)* To stop something from running?

> **ZEKE:** Or to use it somewhere else.

> **CESAR:** *(to Jason)* Does the diagnostic say where they went?

> **JASON:** Destination corrupted. But they went somewhere on this vessel. Which means they're still here.

Three questions. Who. Why. Where.

> **CESAR:** We go back through what we've cleared. We regroup, eat, rest if we need to, and we plan the next section. We're not going deeper without knowing what we're going into.

> **COOPER:** First smart thing anyone's said in the last hour.

> **CESAR:** *(opening comms)* George — heading back to the Operations Center. Hold tight. We have a plan. It's going to take longer than we'd like.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Copy. We're not going anywhere.

> **GEORGE** *(comms — quieter)*: Good work out there.

*They filed back through the blast door gap. The hub stood behind them in red-amber light, three green indicators at the top, four empty housings at the base.*

*The cells were somewhere on the vessel. Someone had moved them deliberately, one day before everything came apart.*

*One thing at a time.*

*The vessel watched them go.*

### — INTERLUDE: THE STATE OF THINGS —

*By 2175, humanity had not conquered space.*

*It had negotiated with it.*

*The terms were unfavorable — they always were — but the species had learned that survival in the void required a particular kind of stubbornness. Not bravery. Not genius. Just the refusal to accept that the conditions were impossible, repeated across generations until the impossible became ordinary, and the ordinary became home.*

*Mars was home now. Cities under domes — hexagonal plexiglass panes fifteen centimeters thick, joined edge to edge across vast spans rising from perimeter walls ten meters high and one and a half meters thick. Inside the dome you would think you were back on Earth. At the outer districts, near the wall, you could see the seams between panes and the dark beyond them and remember exactly where you were.*

*Space travel had improved enough to matter. Eight months from Mars to thirty astronomical units — a distance that once took years — was considered an acceptable posting. The drives were better. The math had improved. What hadn't improved was the dark itself, which was the same dark it had always been, and remained indifferent to human progress.*

*The crew of the survey vessel had made that eight-month trip in a ship running fusion drives at a sustained acceleration of roughly four thousandths of a standard gravity. Peak speed at the midpoint of the journey: approximately one and a half million kilometers per hour — about a tenth of a percent of the speed of light. Gentle, by the standards of what the void required. Enough to get them here.*

*Here being thirty astronomical units from the Sun. Three-quarters of the way to Pluto. The place where a vessel had gone dark thirty-two years ago and the universe had simply continued without it.*

*They were sleeping on the floor of the Operations Center now, five of them, kit lights dimmed, weapons within reach. Outside the blast doors they hadn't locked — because there was no reason to lock them, because nothing on this vessel had given them reason to yet — the facility breathed on its low grid and the terminal on its platform pulsed once, red-amber, in the dark.*

*Slow. Patient.*

*Waiting for morning.*

## PART ONE: THE VOID BETWEEN

---

### The State of Things

By 2175, humanity had not conquered space.

It had negotiated with it.

The terms were unfavorable — they always were — but the species had learned, slowly and at considerable cost, that survival in the void required a particular kind of stubbornness. Not bravery. Not genius. Just the refusal to accept that the conditions were impossible, repeated across generations until the impossible became merely difficult, and the difficult became ordinary, and the ordinary became home.

Mars was home now. Not for everyone. Not comfortably. But home in the way that a place becomes home when enough people are born there and enough people die there and the generations stack up until the original decision to go seems less like a choice and more like a fact of history.

The cities on Mars did not sit under open sky. They sat under domes — vast hexagonal structures of plexiglass panes, each one fifteen centimeters thick and two and a half meters wide, bonded to alloy frames with an epoxy compound that had been refined over decades into something approaching permanence. The panes were joined edge to edge across the full span of the dome, which rose from a perimeter wall ten meters high and one and a half meters thick that circled each city at its boundary like a declaration. Inside the dome you could walk the streets of a city that looked, felt, and smelled like something built on Earth. The light was managed. The temperature was managed. The air was managed. Children grew up in these cities and knew the sky above them was engineered and didn't particularly care, the same way children everywhere have always not particularly cared about the infrastructure that keeps them alive.

At the outer districts, near the wall, you could see the seams between panes. You could look up and count them, hexagon after hexagon, and follow them to the apex of the dome and then back down the other side, and if you stood there long enough in the right light you could see the dark beyond them — not the dark of a cloudy night but the dark of a sky that had no atmosphere to scatter it, a darkness so complete it had weight. Most people who grew up on Mars learned not to look up at the outer districts. Not because it frightened them. Because it reminded them of something they preferred not to think about.

The something was simple: outside the dome, you died in ninety seconds.

Space travel, by 2175, had improved enough to matter. The drives that moved ships through the void were not the drives of a century prior — they were faster, more efficient, less catastrophically expensive to operate. A journey that once took years now took months. This was not magic. It was engineering, accumulated over generations, paid for in failures that nobody put on the brochure. The result was a humanity that could reach the outer solar system in a timeframe that didn't require cryo sleep for the entire voyage. Eight months from Mars to the region of Pluto's orbit was considered an acceptable posting. Not comfortable. But acceptable.

This mattered because of what was out there.

### The Vessel

It had no proper name yet in this telling. Names come later, when things are understood well enough to be called something. For now it was what it had always been to those who didn't live on it: a designation on a corporate manifest, a line item in a budget, a signal on a long-range scan that should have been there and then wasn't.

To the people who lived on it, it was simply the ship. The way a town is simply the town when it's the only one you know.

Construction began in the first quarter of the twenty-first century, in orbit — because it had to be in orbit. The vessel was too large to build under gravity and survive the lifting of it. It was assembled over years from components launched from Earth and later from the earliest Mars facilities, bolted together piece by piece in the patient way that large things get built when there is no alternative to patience. When it was finished it was not beautiful. It was functional, which for something that size was its own kind of beauty. A structure designed to sustain a living, working community for decades without resupply. Self-sufficient in the ways that mattered: power, atmosphere, food production, water cycling, medical care. A city that could move, slowly, through the dark.

It launched in 2093, carrying its first full crew complement, bound for Pluto.

Pluto was not the destination for any romantic reason. Pluto was where the research required it to go — the outer system, far enough from the sun that certain conditions existed nowhere else, close enough to the ecliptic plane that the journey, while long, was calculable. The company that owned and operated the vessel had contracted with several research institutions and government bodies whose specific interests in the outer system were not publicized and were not the business of the general crew. Deep space research was like that. The people who funded it rarely explained themselves to the people who did it.

The journey to Pluto, at the vessel's sustainable cruise speed, would take approximately sixty-seven years.

This was understood from the beginning. Nobody signed on expecting to see Pluto in their lifetime. They signed on for eight-year terms — the minimum period before a rotation ship would come to collect those whose contracts had expired and deliver the next cohort. Eight years was a long time in a small world. Some people did one term and left. Some did two, three, four. Some stopped counting and stopped leaving. The vessel became the thing their lives were built around, the way a city does for the people who grow old in it without particularly meaning to.

Every three to five years a supply ship made the journey. It brought equipment, food stores, replacement parts, medical supplies, new crew, mail from people back on Earth or Mars who were still thinking of someone out here. It brought, occasionally and unofficially, things that weren't on the manifest — small comforts that the long dark made worth the risk of smuggling. Nobody inspected too carefully. The ship's administration understood that a community eight years from the nearest port needed certain allowances to remain functional.

The supply ship would dock, unload, load the outbound crew whose terms had ended, and leave. The journey back took years. By the time news from the vessel reached Earth, it was old news. By the time a response reached the vessel, the situation that prompted it had long since resolved itself one way or another.

This was the texture of life at that distance. You were on your own, in ways that the word didn't quite capture. The people who thrived out there were the ones who made peace with that. Who found the community aboard the vessel to be sufficient. Who stopped measuring their lives against what they'd left behind.

Many of them had nothing to go back to anyway.

By 2143, the vessel was fifty years into its journey. It had carried hundreds of people through its corridors over those decades — scientists, engineers, maintenance workers, medical staff, administrators, and a small number of people who had simply ended up there by a series of decisions that seemed logical at the time. Its population at any given point hovered around one hundred and fifty. A small city's worth of people, moving slowly through the dark toward a destination none of them would live to see.

Three-quarters of the way to Pluto.

That was as far as it got.

### 2143

What happened in 2143 is documented in fragments.

The full picture does not exist in any one place. It exists in pieces — in log entries and sensor records and the preserved data of a ship's systems that kept running long after the people who built them were gone. It exists in the architecture of the disaster itself, in the shape of things left behind, in the empty spaces where things should have been.

What is known: there was research being conducted aboard the vessel that most of its crew knew very little about. This was not unusual. The vessel carried multiple research programs simultaneously, and compartmentalization was standard practice. The one hundred and fifty people who called the vessel home went about their days — maintenance shifts, meal rotations, the small social structures that communities build to make large spaces feel smaller — largely unaware of what was happening in the sealed sections of the research wing. There were whispers, as there always are. Something strange. Something that didn't quite fit the profile of ordinary deep-space science. But whispers in a closed community are easy to dismiss, and most people dismissed them.

The research team at the center of it numbered fourteen. Specialists, brought aboard specifically for this work. They had been on the vessel long enough to be known, to have become part of the fabric of the community, to have faces and habits and histories that other crew members recognized in passing. Whatever they were doing behind the closed doors of their section, they were not strangers.

Something went wrong.

Not all at once. Nothing catastrophic ever happens all at once — it happens in stages, each one survivable in isolation, each one making the next one worse. The vessel had procedures for emergencies. It had redundancies and failsafes and a chain of command designed to manage crisis. These things helped and then they didn't.

By the time the wider crew understood the scale of what they were dealing with, the situation had already progressed beyond what procedures were designed to handle.

The chaos that followed was the kind that happens when a hundred and fifty people in a sealed environment realize simultaneously that their survival is not guaranteed. Resources were contested. Escape was contested. Every decision became a negotiation conducted under conditions that made rational negotiation nearly impossible. People acted out of fear and exhaustion and the particular desperation of knowing that help was years away.

Some survived the initial chaos. Some did not. The escape pod bays saw what they saw — twenty pods, distributed across four bays throughout the vessel, each one built to carry six people away from a ship in crisis. Capacity for one hundred and twenty out of a population of one hundred and fifty. The mathematics of that shortfall played out the way mathematics always plays out: without sentiment.

Between Stardate 2143.162 and 2143.164, pods launched from the bays that people could reach. Not all bays. Not all pods. Chaos is not organized. Some pods launched with fewer than six. Some bays went unreached.

Nine days before the first pod launched, at Stardate 2143.159, the vessel's outgoing transmission systems went offline.

Not from damage. Not from power failure.

Someone turned them off.

The pods that left the vessel left in silence. Their emergency beacons, their distress signals, their navigation transponders — all of them subject to the same system that had been disabled. They drifted away from the vessel and disappeared into the dark the way things disappear at that distance: completely, and without announcement.

The vessel went dark.

The company waited for a routine communication that never came. Then another. They revised their expectations downward: communication failure at extreme range was not unprecedented. The vessel's self-sufficiency meant it didn't need to report regularly to continue functioning. A supply run was several years out. They would know more then.

They noted the anomaly in the operational log.

They moved on.

### The Years Between

Thirty-two years is a long time for a secret to keep itself.

The vessel drifted on its original heading — still pointed at Pluto, still moving, still running on the minimal power its surviving systems could maintain. From the outside, it was indistinguishable from a vessel that had simply gone quiet. Deep space had a way of swallowing things. Ships went dark. Signals decayed. The distance between a vessel at thirty astronomical units and the nearest inhabited body was measured in years of light, in decades of radio. The company restructured twice. Changed hands once. The people who had filed the anomaly report were mostly retired or dead.

The escaped pods drifted in silence somewhere in the outer system, each one carrying whoever had made it aboard, sealed in cryo sleep that would hold for perhaps fifty years at full power before the systems began to fail. They had no signal. They had no way to be found. They were simply out there — suspended, waiting, unknowing.

Thirty-two years passed.

Then, in 2175, the vessel appeared on long-range scans.

Not reappeared. Appeared — as though for the first time, as though it had been invisible and had chosen to stop being invisible. A return signal where there had been none. A location where only silence had been. The scan data was cross-referenced against registry records and matched to the vessel's identification signature, flagged years prior as dark, status unknown.

A company called Hugo Avila.

Hugo called a crew.

### I. THE APPROACH

*The forward viewport of their vessel filled with it slowly, the way bad things tend to — not all at once, but incrementally, until it was simply there and there was no pretending it wasn't.*

*The derelict. Dark. Enormous. No running lights, no beacon, no sign of cycle. A structure designed to sustain one hundred and fifty people indefinitely, sitting dead in the black like someone had simply walked away from it mid-sentence.*

*Stardate 2175.150.0213. Eight months out from Mars.*

---

Jaime Rodriguez keyed the comms for the third time. Same tone as the first two — steady, professional, the kind of voice that didn't telegraph anxiety even when it had good reason to.

> **JAIME:** This is survey vessel on approach — please respond on standard or emergency channel. Registered salvage crew. Respond at your convenience.

Static. Long and flat.

> **COOPER:** "At your convenience." You're very polite to a dark ship, man.

> **JAIME:** You never know.

> **COOPER:** I feel like in this specific case we know.

Ezekiel Tagle was at the sensor array. He'd been quiet since they came into visual range, which meant he was actually thinking.

> **ZEKE:** Hull reads intact. Primary dock corridor is pressurized. Internal atmosphere — present. Most of the facility.

> **JAIME:** Most?

> **ZEKE:** A few sections aren't reading. Could be isolation seals. Could be something else. I'd need to be inside to tell the difference.

Cesar Roque set down his tablet and looked at the viewport.

> **CESAR:** Automated emergency broadcast should be transmitting regardless of crew status. It isn't. That's the part that doesn't fit.

> **COOPER:** So the backup plan also stopped. That's a great sign.

> **CESAR:** It isn't.

> **COOPER:** I know. I was being sarcastic.

> **CESAR:** I know. I was being accurate.

From the comms board, Robert Tijerina came through — calm and measured in the particular way that meant he'd been sitting with something and decided to weigh in.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: What's the read from out there? Hugo's directive flagged automated systems as a priority check before we dock.

> **CESAR:** Emergency broadcast is inactive. Atmosphere and hull are nominal. We're assessing.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Copy. Jason — you've seen dark configurations before. Anything about this that concerns you?

Jason Betancourt was at the secondary panel, running the docking approach calculations he'd already run twice.

> **JASON:** The broadcast being down is the only thing that doesn't add up. Everything else looks like a passive shutdown, not a catastrophic one. We won't know more until we're in.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Right, good. That's the expert read I was looking for.

George Castañeda shifted at the secondary comms board.

> **GEORGE:** I've got nothing on return signal. Still just noise.

> **COOPER:** Were you asleep?

> **GEORGE:** I was conserving energy. It's a long approach.

> **LESSLY** *(from somewhere behind him)*: He was asleep.

> **GEORGE:** *(not denying it)* I heard everything.

Maritza Garza leaned against the far bulkhead, scrolling through facility schematics on her tablet without looking up.

> **MARITZA:** The mechanical systems on this thing are going to be a disaster. Fourteen months dark, no cycle maintenance — whatever's still running in there, it's running wrong.

> **LESSLY:** Are we factoring that into the approach plan or just noting it?

> **MARITZA:** *(still not looking up)* Noting it. Nobody asked me about the approach plan.

> **LESSLY:** Nobody asked me either.

> **MARITZA:** Welcome to the team.

Then, from behind them:

> **ROBERT:** Alright. Let's go over the directive one more time so we're all aligned.

*Nobody turned around immediately.*

> **ROBERT:** Hugo was clear — primary objective is resource recovery, secondary is facility assessment, minimal risk posture throughout. He expects a status report before we lose relay range, which —

> **CESAR:** We know the directive, Robert.

> **ROBERT:** I'm just making sure we're all on the same page. That's part of my role here.

> **CESAR:** We read it. Same as you.

> **ROBERT:** Right, and as the operations lead on this —

> **CESAR:** *(evenly)* The boarding team is Jason, Jaime, Cooper, Zeke, and myself. The plan is to dock, assess the facility entrance, and proceed from the airlock. We've reviewed it.

> **ROBERT:** Yeah, absolutely — though I'd maybe recommend we consider —

> **CESAR:** We're docking, Robert.

Silence. Robert adjusted his tablet.

> **ROBERT:** Hugo also wanted me to flag that if we encounter any technical anomalies — we defer to the *experts* on the team rather than making field calls independently.

> **COOPER:** *(quietly, to Zeke)* The experts.

> **ZEKE:** *(same volume)* I heard it too.

> **ROBERT:** Jason, you've seen more derelict configurations than anyone, so if something comes up —

> **JASON:** I know.

> **ROBERT:** Right, exactly, which is why —

> **JASON:** Robert. I know.

Another silence. George coughed. Lessly found something very interesting to look at on her panel.

> **MARITZA:** *(finally looking up)* The auxiliary power grid's in the rear section of the facility. If anything's still cycling it's coming from there.

> **JASON:** Good to know. Thanks, Maritza.

> **MARITZA:** Sure.

> **ROBERT:** See, that's exactly the kind of expert insight —

> **MARITZA:** Robert, I already have one person I didn't ask to work for. You don't need to compliment me for reading a schematic.

*George made a short sound that might have been a laugh.*

> **GEORGE:** *(to no one in particular)* Every time.

> **LESSLY:** Every time.

The docking alert chimed. Green across the board.

> **CESAR:** We're up. Jaime, Jason, Zeke — dock corridor in three. Cooper, you're with us.

> **COOPER:** *(exhales)* Yeah. I'm with you.

> **JAIME:** *(clapping him once on the shoulder)* It's fine.

> **COOPER:** You don't know that.

> **JAIME:** Not yet.

> **ROBERT:** *(calling after them)* Keep comms open. I'll be coordinating from here. If you need guidance on anything —

*Cesar stopped. Turned back. Looked at Robert — not long, not heated. Just the kind of look that filed something away — then turned back around and kept walking.*

> **GEORGE:** *(once they were gone, quieter)* You all be careful in there.

*The airlock sealed.*

### II. THE CROSSING

The dock corridor between vessels was forty feet of pressurized tube. The facility's airlock accepted their override on the first attempt — no resistance, no alarm. It simply opened.

Which was either good or the kind of bad that didn't bother announcing itself.

Emergency strips ran along the corridor floor — pale amber, low draw, just enough to navigate by. The hum that every inhabited vessel had, that constant low frequency you stopped noticing after the first day — gone. The facility's silence was a different thing from ordinary quiet. It had mass.

> **COOPER:** *(low)* This is fine. Completely normal abandoned space facility.

Nobody replied. They moved.

The corridor was thirty-two years of undisturbed dust on every surface not in their path. The air was old — stale, flat, the atmosphere of a sealed space that had been breathing itself for decades. It was breathable. It just didn't feel like it wanted to be.

---

### III. THE COMMAND CENTER

*The Operations Center was wide and tiered, workstations arranged in arcs facing a primary display wall that had been dark for thirty-two years. At the center of the room, raised on a platform: the main terminal. One indicator light pulsed on its housing. Slow. Patient.*

*One hundred and fifty people had worked on this vessel.*

*None of them were here.*

---

Cesar walked the perimeter first — exits, entry points, adjacencies. The others spread naturally: Jaime at a side workstation, trying for local logs. Zeke straight to the terminal. Jason taking in the room.

> **ZEKE:** It's active. Protected state, but the interface is live. It's been waiting for input.

> **CESAR:** What does it want?

> **ZEKE:** A user.

Cesar looked at Jason.

> **CESAR:** What's your read?

> **JASON:** Could be a standard idle lock. Touch it and it either opens a guest layer or asks for credentials. Either way we learn something. Could also be a trip — depends on how the system was configured when it went down.

> **CESAR:** *(a beat)* Zeke — try the guest layer. Don't push past any access prompts. Anything that asks for credentials, you stop and we read it first.

> **ZEKE:** Got it.

Comms crackled.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Status update. Hugo wanted a check-in before you interact with anything in the Operations Center.

> **CESAR:** We're at the main terminal. About to try guest access. Atmosphere is stable, room is clear.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Copy. Hugo flagged automated security — directive was not to trigger any lockout protocols.

> **CESAR:** We're aware.

> **COOPER:** *(to Jason, low)*: Every time.

> **JASON:** *(same volume)*: Every time.

Zeke touched the terminal.

*The display came alive. Flickered. Resolved. Text populated line by line — deliberate, like something waking up and choosing its words.*

```

v4.2.6

WELCOME BACK.

INITIATING FACILITY STATUS REPORT...
```

> **JAIME:** *(watching the screen)* It's been counting days.

> **ZEKE:** It logged us the moment we crossed the airlock. Probably before.

The status report ran. Power levels, atmospheric data, system states. Then:

```

PERSONNEL — CURRENT:             [CLASSIFIED]
```

> **COOPER:** Current personnel — classified. What does that mean?

> **JASON:** It means the system knows something it isn't telling us.

> **CESAR:** Zeke, pull the archive access prompt. Let's see what clearance level it's asking for before we do anything else.

Zeke reached for the input field.

The screen changed.

```
THIS IS NOT A WARNING.
```

### IV. DARK

The lights went out.

Not dim. Not flicker. Out — every display, every emergency strip, every indicator on every panel, simultaneously, completely. The ventilation stopped. The low background hum of the facility confirmed itself as gone.

Four seconds of absolute dark and absolute silence.

> **COOPER:** *(somewhere in the black)* Nobody move.

> **JAIME:** I'm here — I've got a light —

Kit lights came on. Three beams finding each other, finding the walls, finding the terminal — dark now, no pulse, no indicator.

> **ZEKE:** Full shutdown. Not protected state. Root-level kill.

> **CESAR:** *(already on comms)* George — status.

> **GEORGE** *(comms — different now)*: We lost everything. Power, displays, dock controls. All of it, same moment. We're on backup air.

> **MARITZA** *(comms, background)*: Mechanical grid is completely dead. I've got nothing to work with.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: What happened in there? The directive said —

> **CESAR:** Robert. Dock release status.

> **ROBERT:** It's — not responding. Lessly —

> **LESSLY** *(comms)*: Dock clamps are locked. I can't release from our side. It has to come from the facility or we need full power to override. We're not going anywhere.

Silence on both ends.

> **JASON:** *(quietly, to Cesar)* The docking protocol pulled power from our ship. Standard handshake — fuel cell charge cycle, the usual when a vessel berths. Nothing connected on the data side. Power was drawn anyway. Our ship drained in the attempt.

> **CESAR:** So it wasn't targeted.

> **JASON:** Effect is the same. We're not going anywhere until we restore enough of the facility's grid to run the clamp sequence.

The emergency grid came back. Lower than anything they'd seen. A pale, cold blue-white trace along ceiling seams and floor edges. Life support. Minimal lighting. Communications.

That was all.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Low power's back. Air, lights, comms. Dock release still locked.

> **MARITZA** *(comms)*: I need the mechanical grid before I can do anything from here.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Okay. What's the move?

> **CESAR:** We find the facility's rear power supply, assess what it needs, and restore enough grid to release the clamps. We can't do that from where you are.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: You can do that?

> **JASON:** We can work the problem. That's all I'll tell you right now.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Right. Hugo's going to want —

> **CESAR:** Robert. Stay on channel. Stay calm. We'll report when we have something.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: ...Copy.

> **GEORGE** *(comms — quieter)*: Hey. You all be careful.

> **CESAR:** We will, George.

### V. INTO THE DARK

The low grid ended at the corridor threshold like a boundary someone had drawn on purpose. Beyond it — nothing. The kind of dark that a kit light made a narrow tunnel through and no more.

Cesar went first.

They moved single file through a vessel that was trying to remember how to be a vessel. Cesar with his light forward, Jason behind him, Jaime running the map on his kit screen, Zeke at the rear. Cooper fell in between Zeke and Jaime, which put him exactly in the middle.

> **JAIME:** *(low, checking the map)* Branch left takes us toward Cargo and Engineering. Right runs parallel to Crew Quarters. Straight on is the longer route to the rear grid.

> **CESAR:** Any of those sections flagged on the sensor read?

> **JAIME:** Cargo and Engineering are partial. Crew Quarters didn't register at all.

> **JASON:** That's the section that wasn't on the low grid.

> **CESAR:** We go straight. We don't open doors we don't have to.

The corridor widened briefly at a transit junction — a lift lobby, doors closed and dark. Against the wall, a supply cart sat abandoned, halfway loaded, one wheel ticking against the deck in slow oscillation from the low-power vibration in the plating.

> **COOPER:** *(passing)* Someone left in a hurry.

> **ZEKE:** Or didn't leave at all.

Cooper kept walking.

Further on, the low grid resumed intermittently. A door to the left stood open — panel green, opened from inside and left that way.

Cesar stopped at it. Looked at Jason.

> **CESAR:** What's your read?

> **JASON:** Engineering or Maintenance, based on position. Could have what we need to assess the power grid. Could also tell us what happened in here.

> **CESAR:** We go in. Everybody stays in the threshold sightline — nobody past ten feet until I say.

A maintenance bay. Tool racks along the walls, most still stocked. A workbench with a personal tablet face-down. One overhead panel partially collapsed from thirty-two years of no one to maintain it.

Zeke crossed to the workbench without being asked. Tried the tablet. Dead battery.

> **ZEKE:** Dead. Battery's gone.

> **JAIME:** *(looking at the tool racks)* Most of this is still here. They weren't looting supplies when it happened.

> **JASON:** Whatever it was, it wasn't slow enough to pack.

He moved to the primary systems panel on the far wall. Display dark, but one physical indicator — amber. Active.

His hand moved briefly to the holster at his hip without thinking about it. Old habit in an unfamiliar room. He didn't draw. Just noted it was there and moved on.

> **JASON:** This section's on the grid. Low draw, but active. Rear supply is still running something. We find it, there's something to work with.

> **CESAR:** Good enough. Let's keep moving.

> **COOPER:** *(from the threshold, not having moved)* Are we not going to talk about the fact that the door was already open?

> **CESAR:** It was left open.

> **COOPER:** By who?

A beat.

> **CESAR:** That's what we're trying to find out.

They moved on. The corridor continued — sealed cargo bay they didn't open, another junction, a long straight stretch the kit lights couldn't fully reach.

Comms crackled.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Still with us?

> **CESAR:** Still moving. Working toward the rear power supply. Nothing to report yet.

> **GEORGE:** We're sitting at backup air and comms. Maritza's been going through the schematics.

> **MARITZA** *(comms, background)*: The rear supply feeds two sub-grids. If you can get either one back up it might be enough to restore dock function. Might.

> **JASON:** What does it take to bring a sub-grid online?

> **MARITZA:** Without knowing what killed it? Could be a manual reset. Could be hardware. Could be something else.

> **JASON:** Could be something else.

> **MARITZA:** That's the one I'd prepare for.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: I want to flag — Hugo's going to ask for a status report when we hit relay range —

> **CESAR:** Robert, when we have something to report we'll report it.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: ...Copy.

> **GEORGE* *(comms)*: You'll find it. Go on.

The rear section opened ahead — the low grid resuming at something approaching usable. A wide set of pressure doors at the end of the corridor, closed but panel green.

### VII. CARGO

*The door to the cargo bay wasn't open.*

*The door to the cargo bay was gone.*

*Not forced. Removed from its track and left leaning against the corridor wall, deliberately, like someone had needed the opening wider and hadn't come back to put it right.*

---

The space inside was large — shelving units in rows, floor to ceiling. Some fully stocked. Others stripped bare. Others partially emptied, remaining contents pushed aside, knocked over, abandoned mid-sort. Crates on the floor, some open, some broken. In the main aisle a utility dolly on its side, one wheel ticking against the deck from the low-power vibration in the plating.

> **COOPER:** *(watching the wheel)* That can't still be moving.

> **ZEKE:** Settlement. Deck plates flex on low power.

> **COOPER:** Sure.

Jaime moved to the nearest intact shelf, scanning what remained. He recognized the pattern immediately — what was missing versus what was left.

> **JAIME:** They took what they could carry. Anything hand-portable is gone. Anything that needed the dolly is still here.

> **JASON:** Or they ran out of time to come back.

> **JAIME:** Yeah. Or that.

Cesar moved through the main aisle. At the third side aisle he stopped.

> **CESAR:** In here.

*A sleeping bag. Rolled out between two shelving units in a gap just wide enough for a person. A water container. A ration pack opened, half-eaten, set aside with the intention of finishing it later.*

*Nobody had finished it later.*

> **COOPER:** Someone was hiding in here.

> **JASON:** More than once. Look at the scuff marks.

The marks were clear in the kit light — the kind that built up over days of the same movement. Someone had returned to this spot repeatedly. Used it to sleep, to wait, to keep existing in a ship that had stopped being safe.

> **JAIME:** Did they make it to a pod?

No answer.

Jason moved to the far wall where heavy equipment had been left. Generator housings, both dead. His hand moved briefly to his holster — the same absent gesture, the same confirmation — and moved on.

> **JASON:** Generator housings back here — both dead. If we restore the rear grid we can cycle them.

### VIII. CREW QUARTERS

*The corridor narrowed. Ceiling dropped. Infrastructure simplified. This part of the vessel had been built for people to live in rather than work in. The distinction was architectural and it was something else as well — something that settled over the group as they moved into it without anyone naming it.*

*Four doors on each side. Most closed. Two open.*

---

Cesar held up a hand. They stopped.

Nothing moved. The low grid cast pale light on eight doors and thirty-two years of accumulated quiet.

> **CESAR:** Two minutes. Don't go past the doorframe alone.

They split. Cesar and Jason took the first open door left. Zeke and Jaime took the one across. Cooper stayed at the junction, hand resting on the grip of his pistol — not drawing, just resting. The thing you did in a corridor when everyone else was in a room.

*Inside the first quarter: two bunks. One made with the precision of long habit. The other slept in and not remade — sheets pulled back, pillow still bearing the impression of a head. A mug on the desk, something long-dried in the bottom. A photograph fixed to the wall. Two people. Somewhere with sunlight.*

Jason looked at the mug. Looked at the photograph. Didn't touch either.

> **JASON:** *(low)* Someone made their bed every morning. Someone else didn't.

> **CESAR:** Thirty-two years and that's still the first thing you notice.

> **JASON:** Tells you something about who they were.

Across the corridor, Jaime stood in the doorway without going in. Zeke was at the desk, checking a personal device. Dead battery.

> **JAIME:** *(quietly)* They had full lives here. Pictures. Coffee. Bad habits.

> **ZEKE:** Forty-seven days from the nearest port, based on the mission profile. They weren't going home soon.

> **JAIME:** No.

> **JAIME:** You think they had any warning? Before it got bad?

> **ZEKE:** The door came down in cargo on 2143.169. Pods launched 2143.162 to 2143.164. Whatever happened, it moved fast at first and then lasted a long time after.

> **JAIME:** The person in the cargo bay. To at least 2143.181.

> **ZEKE:** Minimum.

Jaime looked at the photograph on the wall across the hall. From where he stood it was just shape and light.

> **JAIME:** Okay.

He stepped back from the doorframe.

> **COOPER:** *(from down the corridor, quiet)* Hey. There's something here.

*At the far end of the quarters corridor, half-recessed in a maintenance alcove: a personal kit bag. Packed. Zipped. Set carefully against the wall. A faded name tab on the shoulder strap.*

*Next to it, tucked under the strap: a child's drawing. Folded twice.*

*Nobody unfolded it.*

> **COOPER:** *(long moment, no humor in it)* I don't like this place.

> **JAIME:** I know.

> **CESAR:** Mark the section. We keep moving.

Cooper looked at the bag one more time. Adjusted the grip of his pistol — the same absent gesture as Jason in the maintenance bay and cargo hold, the same quiet confirmation — and turned back to the corridor.

> **COOPER:** Yeah. Okay.

### IX. THE REAR POWER SUPPLY

*The corridor narrowed once more before widening into the final approach. At the end: blast doors, cracked open just enough for one person at a time. The manual override lever on the frame was bent — used hard, from the inside.*

*Above the doors, the amber indicator pulsed in a long, slow rhythm.*

*Something back there was still running.*

---

> **JASON:** *(seeing the indicator)* There it is.

> **CESAR:** Single file. Jason, you're behind me. Weapons ready — don't draw unless I say.

*They slipped through the gap one at a time.*

*Beyond the blast doors: the hum they'd been moving toward resolved into something physical — a low vibration in the deck plates underfoot. Emergency lighting stronger than anywhere else in the facility. Red-amber, purposeful. The lighting of a room that knew it was important.*

*The space was industrial. Conduit and housing everywhere. At the center: the primary power distribution hub — a column of stacked components twelve feet high, indicator panels running its full height. Most dark. Some amber. Three at the very top — green.*

*Still running.*

*Still waiting.*

Jason was already at the column.

> **JAIME:** *(moving alongside, raising his kit light)* What are we looking at?

> **JASON:** The hub's intact. No physical damage I can see. Those three stacks at the top are what's been running the low grid and life support. Everything below them is dark because there's nothing feeding it.

He crouched at the base. Popped the access panel with the flat of his hand. Inside: four cell housings. All of them empty. Contacts clean. Housing intact.

> **JASON:** They pulled the primary cells.

> **ZEKE:** *(from the secondary diagnostic panel across the room)* Same here. Secondary feed is empty too.

> **CESAR:** Maritza — hub is structurally intact. Primary and secondary cell housings are empty. What does that mean for the sub-grids?

> **MARITZA** *(comms)*: They're not coming back without new power sources. The hub is just a distributor — it doesn't generate anything. You need to feed it fuel cells. Should be distributed in storage throughout the vessel. Engineering bays, cargo, maintenance.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: So you need to find fuel cells. On a vessel that's been dark for thirty-two years.

> **CESAR:** That's what it sounds like.

> **ROBERT** *(comms)*: Hugo's not going to —

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Robert.

Silence from Robert.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Jason. Talk to me. Is it doable?

Jason stood. Looked at the empty housings. Looked up the full height of the hub to the three green indicators still running at the top.

> **JASON:** The vessel's been running on whatever was left in those top stacks for thirty-two years. That means the cell design holds charge for an extremely long time under low draw. If there are spares somewhere on this ship — and a vessel this size, there should be — they're not going to be dead just from sitting. We find them, we bring them back here, we feed the hub.

> **GEORGE:** And the dock release?

> **JASON:** Needs enough grid to run the clamp sequence. Two, maybe three cells minimum to get there.

> **GEORGE:** How many for full power and clear?

> **JASON:** More than three.

Silence on the comms.

> **COOPER:** *(from the blast door gap, quietly)* So we're going deeper into the ship.

It wasn't a question.

> **JASON:** Yeah.

> **COOPER:** Great.

Zeke had something on the diagnostic panel. He read it twice before speaking.

> **ZEKE:** Jason. The cells weren't depleted. Look at the draw history — hard cutoff. Not a drain. They were removed.

Jason crossed the room. Read the panel.

> **JASON:** Stardate 2143.158.

> **ZEKE:** One day before the transmissions were cut.

The room went quiet in a different way than before.

> **CESAR:** Someone pulled the primary power cells on 2143.158. Transmissions went down on 2143.159. Pods launched 2143.162 through 2143.164.

> **ZEKE:** Someone knew what was coming. Before the pods. Before the transmissions. They pulled the power deliberately.

> **JAIME:** *(carefully)* To stop something from running?

> **ZEKE:** Or to use it somewhere else.

> **CESAR:** *(to Jason)* Does the diagnostic say where they went?

> **JASON:** Destination corrupted. But they went somewhere on this vessel. Which means they're still here.

Three questions. Who. Why. Where.

The vessel wasn't answering yet.

> **CESAR:** We go back through what we've cleared. We regroup, eat, rest if we need to, and we plan the next section. We're not going deeper tonight without knowing what we're going into.

> **COOPER:** First smart thing anyone's said in the last hour.

> **CESAR:** *(opening comms)* George — heading back to the Operations Center. Hold tight. We have a plan. It's going to take longer than we'd like.

> **GEORGE** *(comms)*: Copy. We're not going anywhere.

*A pause.*

> **GEORGE** *(comms — quieter)*: Good work out there.

*They filed back through the blast door gap one at a time. The hub stood behind them in the red-amber light, three green indicators running at the top, four empty housings at the base.*

*The cells were somewhere on the vessel.*

*Someone had moved them deliberately, one day before everything came apart.*

*Ahead: the corridor. The quarters. The cargo bay. The pod bay. The Operations Center with the terminal that had been waiting.*

*They had a direction. They didn't have answers yet.*

*One thing at a time.*

*The vessel watched them go.*
